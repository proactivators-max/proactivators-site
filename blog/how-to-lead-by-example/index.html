How to Lead by Example (Without Preaching to Anyone)

There is a man at my weekend basketball game named Gaston. When this story starts, he is 64.

He is not a flashy player. What he is, is savvy. He knows his spots. He makes his threes. I call him Houdini, because the moment I take my eyes off him he disappears and scores, and he has broken my heart with more than one game-winner.

A few years ago he started lifting weights. He shows up at the gym at 5:00 AM. Within three or four months he looked like a different man — stronger, sharper, his face lit up. Today he tells me he feels as good as he did when he was much younger.

I never told him to do it.

That distinction is the entire subject of this post. Most people trying to influence someone reach for words, and words are almost always the weakest tool available. If you want to know how to lead by example, you have to understand what actually moves people — and it is not your argument.

What I Did, and What I Deliberately Did Not Do

Gaston and I became friends the way people do on a court: slowly, sideways, between games. And because my life is built on the Six Pillars, the conversation eventually turned to fitness, habits, and mindset. It always does.

So I told him what I believed. That as we age, we have to make the muscle stronger. That the court alone will not build the foundation his heart and his longevity need. That the iron does something basketball cannot.

Then I stopped.

I did not push. I did not preach. I did not check in the next game to see whether he had thought about it. I said the true thing once, in a moment where he had opened the door, and I left it there.

Nobody has ever been talked into changing their life. They decide, on their own clock, once staying the same finally costs more.

He thought it over. Then one morning I looked up at 5:00 AM and he was there.

Here is what most people get wrong about that story. They think the conversation was the cause. It was not. The conversation was permission. The cause was every game before it, and every game after.

Why Your Effort Gives Other People Permission

In most rooms, mediocrity is the default, and it is enforced socially. People hold back not because they lack the capacity, but because nobody wants to be the try-hard — the one who visibly cares more than everyone else. So the room quietly agrees to operate below its ceiling, and everyone in it privately resents the arrangement.

Then somebody breaks it.

At my CrossFit gym I do not give speeches. I work. I go to the place where it hurts and I hold the standard. And what happens next is predictable enough that I now expect it: the people around me start adding weight to their own bar. On the basketball court, when I bring real intensity, the game speeds up. Everyone plays with more fire.

They are not imitating me. They are being released. My effort tells them one thing: it is okay to try. It is okay to care. It is okay to want more than this.

That is the mechanism. Leading by example is not modeling behavior so others can copy the technique. It is absorbing the social cost of caring, publicly, so the people around you no longer have to pay it.

There Is Something Underneath This

Some of this is wired in. Researchers have identified mirror neurons — brain cells that fire both when you perform an action and when you watch someone else perform it. They are the usual explanation for why a yawn travels across a room, and why you smile back before you have decided to.

I want to be careful here, because this is where motivational content usually overreaches. Mirror neurons explain the small, fast, involuntary stuff well. Whether they are the full neurological basis of leadership is a much bigger claim than the science supports, and I am not going to make it.

You do not need the neuroscience anyway, because you have the experience. You have walked into a room where someone was calm and felt your own shoulders drop. You have sat next to someone anxious and caught it. State is transmissible, and you have felt it from both sides.

Which means the question is not whether you are broadcasting. You are. The question is what.

People Buy Certainty, Not Information

Here is the uncomfortable part.

Nobody follows the best plan. They follow the most certain person. Information is free and infinite and it has never once been the bottleneck. What people are actually scanning you for, mostly below the level of conscious thought, is whether you believe your own material.

And you cannot fake that, which is the good news and the bad news at the same time.

When I am disciplined and nobody is watching — when the 4:00 AM alarm gets obeyed, when I refuse the mental candy, when I treat my family with honor behind a closed door — I am not primarily building a habit. I am sending a signal to my own subconscious: you are real, you have nothing to hide, your private life and your public life match.

That produces a steadiness other people feel across a room, and it cannot be manufactured on the day you need it. It is the residue of every unwitnessed rep — built entry by entry, the way I described in Confidence Is Evidence.

The inverse is just as true. If you are preaching a standard you are not privately keeping, people feel the gap before they can name it. They will not confront you about it. They will just quietly decline to be influenced by you, and you will never find out why.

Why Example Is So Rare Right Now

It is worth understanding the conditions you are operating in, because they are unusually favorable.

The U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory on what it called our epidemic of loneliness and isolation, and reported that the mortality impact of being socially disconnected is comparable to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day. We carry devices that connect us to billions of people, and the loneliness has not moved — because a like is not a hug and a follower count is not a community.

At work, the picture is not better. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace found that roughly one in five employees worldwide was engaged at work in 2025 — down for a second straight year, and the lowest level Gallup has recorded since 2020. Four out of five workers are spending the majority of their waking hours somewhere they have quietly checked out of.

Read those two findings together and you get the actual state of the room you walk into every day: overstimulated and undernourished. Stuffed with content, starving for substance.

Distraction is everywhere. Everyone is selling the shortcut. The other thing — discipline, order, accountability, someone who is actually where they say they are — is rare. When you become a person who is fit, focused, faithful, financially ordered, and fulfilled, you are not competing for attention. You are one of the few people in the room holding bread.

It Goes Both Ways

The part of the Gaston story I like most is where it turns around.

Because the trust was real, he became a listener of the podcast. I had put out an episode on one of the most useful things I have ever learned: that the key to a good day is refusing to let anything bother you. He took it seriously, started guarding his internal state on purpose, and has told me more than once how much it changed the way he moves through a day. He is now telling other people about it.

Then one day he mentioned he had started taking cold showers, morning and afternoon. He had beaten the cold.

I had not. Hearing Gaston say it plainly made me want to do it too. I started, and I beat my own fear of the cold water.

Iron sharpens iron, and the sharpening runs in both directions. If you think of yourself only as the light in the room, you will miss what is being handed back to you. Some of the best upgrades in my life have come from people I thought I was influencing. That reciprocity is the whole logic of living beyond yourself — you do not pour out and go empty. You pour out and get refilled from directions you did not predict.

The Failure Mode: Becoming the Loudspeaker

Everything above collapses if you get the timing wrong.

I do not talk about the pillars with people who did not ask — not out of modesty, out of effectiveness. Unrequested advice does not land as help. It lands as judgment, and it activates every defense the person has. You do not just fail to persuade them; you make them harder to reach next time. Most people who think they are leading by example are running a low-grade campaign, and cannot understand why nobody is converting.

So you wait. And the questions do arrive, on a timeline you do not set. They tend to come sideways, too — not teach me your system, but a half-joke in a parking lot about how you are always up before everyone else, or a quiet hey, can I ask you something after a game.

That is the door. It only opens from the inside, and when it opens you can hand over everything you have — the routine, the framework, the whole blueprint. Until then, you are not being patient. You are being useful in the only register they can currently receive.

That no one is coming to save you is the argument for picking the standard up in the first place; this is what happens to the people around you once you have. Leading a household the same way — by standard, not volume — is in Your Home Is Your First Boardroom.

Field Note — The Unspoken Leadership Audit

Three things you can do this week.

  • Audit the gap. Name one standard you have recommended to someone in the last month. Now be honest about whether you are currently keeping it in private. If the answer is no, stop recommending it and go close the gap. The gap is what people are actually reading.
  • Find the ceiling in one room. Pick a room you are in regularly — a gym, a team, a family table. Identify the unspoken standard everyone is agreeing to stay under. Then break it once, without commentary, and watch what happens to the person next to you.
  • Say the true thing once, then stop. Think of one person you have been quietly trying to fix. Give them the truth one time, cleanly, without a follow-up plan. Then take your hands off it entirely, and go back to being worth imitating. Gaston took time. That was not failure; that was the timeline.

And guard your own inputs while you do it. You cannot broadcast a state you are not in — which is why what you feed your attention is not a side issue but the whole supply line. That is the argument in Focus: The Currency of Your Life.

The Standard Is the Message

Gaston did not change because I convinced him. He changed because he spent time watching someone show up, and one day the cost of staying the same got higher than the cost of starting.

You are already leading by example. Everyone in proximity to you is catching something. The only real question is whether what they are catching is worth having — and that is decided long before anyone is watching, in the reps nobody will ever see.

Gaston, if you are reading this, brother — keep it up.

FAQ — Leading by Example

How long does it take to influence someone by example?

Longer than you want, and it is not on your schedule. Gaston watched me for a while before he walked into that gym, and I had no idea the clock was running. This is the part people cannot tolerate, so they revert to talking — talking feels like progress because you can measure it. Example does not give you feedback until it works. If you need to see the effect in order to keep going, you will quit before it lands.

Isn't this just letting people fail when I could help them?

No — the truth still gets said. The difference is that it gets said once, at a moment when they have opened the door, and then it is released. Repeating it is what turns help into pressure, and pressure is what makes people defend the very thing they were considering giving up. Saying the true thing one time and letting an adult make an adult decision is not neglect. It is respect, and it is also the only version that works.

What if nobody around me ever asks?

Then check two things. First, whether the standard is actually visible — if the discipline happens entirely offstage, there is nothing to catch. Second, whether you have already spent your credibility by preaching, because once someone has filed you as the person who lectures, they stop asking on purpose. If both are clean and nobody asks, keep going anyway. The standard was never contingent on someone noticing it, and you are the primary beneficiary of it either way.

Does leading by example work if I am not the leader on paper?

It works better. Authority makes it easy to dismiss you — people can attribute your standard to your position rather than your character. Someone with no positional power holding an unusually high standard is much harder to explain away, and therefore much harder to ignore. Every room has an informal ceiling that has nothing to do with the org chart, and anyone in the room can break it.

How do I know if I am being the light or the loudspeaker?

Count who started the conversation. If you are consistently the one raising the subject, you are the loudspeaker, whatever you think your intentions are. If people are coming to you with questions you did not prompt, the example is doing its work. That ratio is the honest measurement, and it is uncomfortable enough that most people avoid running it.

What do I do when someone asks and I do not have it all figured out?

Tell them exactly that, then tell them what has actually worked so far. Certainty about your own process is not the same as claiming to have arrived, and people can tell the difference immediately. Nobody is looking for someone who has already arrived; they are looking for someone who is honestly further down the same road. Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose the credibility your consistency earned you.

The people around you are already catching something from you right now.
